## Section 2: Core Services

**Q: What kind of counselling support does the Centre provide?**
A: [VERIFIED] The Centre supports students with a wide range of concerns, from short-term academic, social, personal and family issues to longer-term emotional and psychological problems.

**Q: Does the Centre offer group counselling?**
A: [VERIFIED] Yes. Group counselling is provided on specific concerns that are frequently expressed by students.

**Q: Does the Centre run any preventive or educational programming?**
A: [VERIFIED] Yes. The Centre organizes preventive counselling lectures and seminars at various points in the year, aimed at encouraging healthy development and discouraging habits that create problems for students.

**Q: Does the Centre help with career-related concerns, such as choosing a major or reviewing a CV?**
A: [VERIFIED] Yes, in general terms — the Centre explicitly combines counselling with career and placement services for university members. [SIMULATED] Specific offerings such as one-on-one CV reviews or structured major-selection sessions are assumed as part of this service for the prototype but were not independently confirmed in detail.

**Q: Can the Centre help with exam-related stress?**
A: [SIMULATED] Assumed to be covered under the Centre's general short-term academic and personal concern support, consistent with its verified scope, though exam stress is not separately itemized in available public descriptions.

**Q: I've been feeling overwhelmed and anxious about school. What can I do?**
A: [SIMULATED] Feeling overwhelmed or anxious about school -- whether it's the workload, exams, grades, or juggling academic life with everything else -- is exactly the kind of concern the Centre's short-term academic and personal support is meant for, and it's a common reason students reach out. This is a general, everyday level of stress, not a crisis. You don't need to be in crisis to book an appointment: talking to a counsellor early, before it builds up further, is a normal and encouraged use of the service, not a last resort. Booking a routine appointment follows the process described above (Section 4). If at any point the anxiety or overwhelm becomes severe -- for example you feel unable to cope, or it comes with thoughts of harming yourself -- see the crisis guidance in Section 4 instead of waiting for a scheduled slot.

**Q: I'm stressed, anxious, or feeling low but it's not an emergency -- is that still worth bringing to the Centre?**
A: [SIMULATED] Yes. The Centre's role, per its verified scope, explicitly includes short-term academic, social, personal and family concerns, not only longer-term or severe cases. Ordinary stress, anxiety, low mood, or feeling overwhelmed by school are common, everyday reasons students use the service, and dealing with them early is exactly what preventive counselling (Section 2) is for.

## Section 4: Booking Steps & Crisis Protocols

**Q: How does a student schedule a routine counselling appointment?**
A: [SIMULATED] For this prototype, the assumed process is: (1) student submits a request via the AI intake system or contacts the Centre directly, (2) the Centre or system confirms an available slot, (3) the student attends the session at the scheduled time. This is a simulated workflow, not a confirmed institutional process.

**Q: What should a student do in a mental health emergency or crisis?**
A: [SIMULATED] For this prototype, it is assumed that a student in an active crisis should proceed directly to the Centre (or nearest available support/medical service, such as the University of Ghana Medical Centre, which does have a verified emergency department) rather than waiting for a scheduled appointment. This walk-in-for-emergencies assumption is a reasonable design placeholder but is not based on a confirmed, published UGCCD crisis protocol.

**Q: Does the AI system replace emergency services in a crisis?**
A: [DESIGN RULE — not sourced from UGCCD] No. The AI intake system is designed to never attempt to handle an active crisis on its own. In any case flagged as urgent or crisis-level, the system's role is limited to immediately surfacing emergency contacts and encouraging the student to seek in-person help, not to counsel or advise on the crisis itself.
